I like working on problems where the answer isn't obvious.

A product can have beautiful interfaces and still be difficult to use. It can automate a task and still create more work. It can have all the right features and still fail to earn someone's trust.

My work starts before the interface. I try to understand what people are trying to accomplish, where the friction comes from, what the business needs, and what the product can realistically support.

Then I turn those findings into experiences that are clearer, simpler and easier to act on.

How I work

01
Frame
Understand the problem, users, business context and constraints before touching anything else.
02
Explore
Research, map journeys, identify friction and explore possible directions without committing too early.
03

Evaluate trade-offs and define the experience that makes the most sense — not the most impressive one.
04

05

Test, collaborate, iterate and learn from what actually happens when real people use the product.

Designing products where humans and AI work together.

I'm particularly interested in products where AI doesn't simply generate an output, but participates in a workflow. That means thinking carefully about the boundary between what the machine handles and what the human controls.

What should the AI do?
What should it never do?
How confident does it need to be before acting?
When should a human take over?
How should uncertainty be communicated?
Explore the AI case study →
Principle
Automate confidence, not uncertainty. When the system is confident and the action is low-risk, automate it. When confidence drops or consequences increase, increase human involvement.
Design question
How do you show an AI's uncertainty without making the product feel unnecessarily complicated?
Stop condition
The AI should not behave with more certainty than it has. When it can't confidently determine intent, it asks — or escalates. It never confidently invents an answer.
